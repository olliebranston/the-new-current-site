Gillian O’Boyle on agentic AI for grid operators: achieving growth

Growth opportunities in forecasting, dispatch and planning

Ollie Branston with Gillian O’Boyle · 22 September 2026

AI · Grid operations · Forecasting · Network planning

Britain’s grid is being asked to absorb more generation, more flexible demand and more operational uncertainty without letting cost or connection times drift further out. Agentic AI matters because it changes the tempo of decision-making: better forecasts can inform where networks reinforce, sharper control-room tools can reduce reliance on blunt reserve margins, and faster planning can help operators test more scenarios before committing scarce capital. The opportunity is therefore larger than clerical efficiency. It is a way to increase system throughput in a power market where manual processes are becoming a constraint. The catch is organisational rather than purely technical. Without usable data, clear accountability, cyber controls and a redesigned operating model, agents will remain stranded pilots. Operators who can overcome this will develop a material competitive advantage.

An integrated part of the transition

Britain’s power system is becoming more decentralised, more data-rich and more operationally volatile. In a previous TNC article, the difficulty in getting grid operators from zero to one was explored. Since then, the scale of the opportunity has made the next phase harder to avoid. The joint DESNZ-Ofgem Energy Digitalisation Framework is explicit that digitalisation is now the foundation for artificial intelligence, forecasting and system decision-making.

As such, agentic AI has become part of the attempt to operate a more complex electricity system at lower cost and higher speed than manual processes can sustain.

Seeing the opportunities beyond clerical efficiency

The headcount implications of agentic AI for grid operators have already been discussed. But the potential upside goes beyond labour substitution: Whilst software can summarise documents, triage inboxes and save minutes in workflows as a copilot, the larger benefit is realised when network operators, system operators and utilities can raise the number and quality of decisions they make each day without increasing headcount in proportion.

Faster planning, tighter dispatch, better targeting of capital and more effective use of flexibility provide bigger gains than clerical automation. Ofgem’s own framing is telling here: AI has a role in planning, management and real-time operation, but only within a governance model that protects consumers and keeps accountability visible. Three value pools stand out: forecasting, real-time operation and network planning.

Forecasting

Forecasting changes both long-horizon planning and day-to-day operations. Better demand and supply forecasts influence where infrastructure is reinforced, where flexibility is procured and how reserve is set. They also matter because the data problem is getting harder. Behind-the-meter solar, battery behaviour, electric vehicle charging and changing weather patterns mean the network increasingly lacks visibility of some of the things it must still plan around.

The market is already moving in this direction. NESO’s earlier collaboration with the Alan Turing Institute delivered a 33 per cent improvement in solar forecasting. UKPN is aiming to use AI to identify ‘invisible’ solar behind the meter so it can avoid unnecessary network upgrades or flexibility spend.

Better forecasts provide commercial value by reducing avoidable capital expenditure, sharpening flexibility procurement and improving the use of existing assets before committing new investment.

Real-time operation

The next step is converting forecast quality into operational action. If operators can dispatch batteries, flexible units and reserve more precisely, they can reduce the cost of balancing and improve resilience without over-procuring expensive back-up.

NESO’s Dynamic Reserve Setting project is using machine learning to set reserve dynamically a day ahead, while its Advanced Dispatch Optimisation work is intended to improve forecasting, dispatch, monitoring and the management of increasingly complex grid operations.

This has significant commercial impact. NESO spent £2.7bn balancing Great Britain’s power system in 2024-25. Agentic AI will not make balancing costs disappear. It can, however, make the system less dependent on blunt reserve margins and manual dispatch choices when better information is available.

Planning

Utilities and network operators face a much-publicised throughput problem, with too many projects, too many scenarios and too much uncertainty for linear planning processes to cope elegantly. Ofgem’s latest infrastructure snapshot said the queue had stretched to 750GW by late 2024 and early 2025, with average waits approaching six years.

Here, agentic AI can move beyond an efficiency benefit to become a growth lever. SSEN’s FastTrack project is developing AI to simulate the effect of connection requests across the network so planners can make faster decisions on future investment and improve connection times. SP Energy Networks’ AI-led digital twin project ‘ENSIGN’ is designed to model how rising electricity demand can be managed on a live network counterpart and identify cost-effective network solutions.

The common thread is a higher planning tempo: more scenarios tested, more connection requests assessed and fewer capital decisions made with partial visibility. The agentic layer emerges when software orchestrates these tools across a full workflow.

How to implement the change

Implementation starts with the operating model.

Grid operators should begin by defining the commercial or system outcomes they care about: lower balancing costs, faster connections, better flexibility procurement or improved network planning. Only then should they ask what data, controls and capabilities those outcomes require.

As previously described, getting to AI-ready data can prove a challenge. Access to the right data is critical for AI in the energy system, and the Energy Digitalisation Framework argues that without interoperable data and common standards, AI becomes fragmented, opaque and difficult to oversee. This makes usable, governed data the next implementation hurdle.

The third step is the operating-model redesign itself. Agentic AI shifts the management unit from the process to the outcome. Agents do not erase accountability but make it more explicit: someone still owns flexibility, dispatch, planning or customer impact. The difference is that the accountable owner increasingly supervises a machine-supported workflow rather than manually executing tasks.

If agents are treated as a workforce, they need the disciplines of one: supervision, audit trails, access controls, performance monitoring and escalation routes. That requires agent governance, model assurance, data stewardship, cyber control and new capability models.

This is where pilots tend to become stranded. Ofgem’s guidance is clear that AI deployment in the sector needs governance and accountability, risk mitigation, competence and cyber controls. It requires organisations to absorb new decision rights without losing trust, oversight or operational discipline.

The final step is test-and-learn deployment. Agents should run in parallel with business-as-usual, rather than targeting an immediate transformation. This is particularly relevant for critical national infrastructure: parallel processing first, confidence second, and widened permissions only after repeated outperformance and governance sign-off.

What changes next?

In regulated networks, this will move quickly from differentiator to table stakes. Early movers can improve planning, dispatch and cost performance first, but in a price-controlled sector, good practice rarely stays proprietary for long. If one operator uses AI to improve forecast accuracy, accelerate connections or avoid unnecessary spend, regulators and peers will eventually treat that capability as the new baseline.

ED3 is the next electricity distribution price control, covering April 2028 to March 2033. It will set the revenue allowances, performance incentives and delivery expectations for Distribution Network Operators during a period when local grids must connect more low-carbon generation, support more flexible demand and manage a more complex edge of the system. Ofgem has already been explicit that smarter networks will require stronger data-sharing and processing capabilities, with digitalisation tied to how DNOs plan and operate their businesses.

The next proof points are therefore practical rather than philosophical: measurable forecast improvements, lower reserve procurement, better use of flexibility, faster connections and visible operating-model changes inside the organisations adopting these tools. The case weakens if data remains too fragmented, if cyber or governance failures dent trust, or if companies deploy AI without redesigning accountability around it. It strengthens if operators can show that AI reduces operating costs, shortens connection timelines or improves utilisation of existing network capacity.

Quick-fire industry review

What is the best near-term commercial opportunity in the space?

Demand and supply forecasting. It is the clearest route from better data to better planning, lower reserve needs and more efficient use of flexibility.

What is the single biggest barrier?

Data quality and accessibility, followed closely by organisational adoption.

What policy change would help the industry the most?

Faster progress on shared digital infrastructure and safe test-and-learn conditions. The sector needs data-sharing arrangements and governance that make cross-organisation easier.

What is the most overhyped misconception today?

That utilities can keep the same operating model and constraints. The harder work sits in data, governance, accountability and process redesign.

Which one metric would you track monthly for the industry?

Consumer participation in flexibility and smart-tariff behaviour. If digital and AI tools are working properly, they should result in easier, more automated demand shifting at the edge of the system.

For those interested, where can they learn more?

NESO, Ofgem and DESNZ have put out publications on digitalisation, balancing, connections and AI guidance. Innovation pages of the major network operators are also interesting to see where the use cases are moving from concept to deployment.
