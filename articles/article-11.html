Andrew Perkins and Thomas Fletcher on the rise of energy regulation in GB

GB power-market regulation: which risks the market can price, and which it cannot

Jul 2026 · Energy market regulation · Power markets · CfDs and RABs · Energy transition

Britain's power market is becoming more regulated as the energy transition changes what must be financed. As wind, solar, nuclear, networks and storage play a larger role, system economics shift from fuel-heavy operating costs towards upfront capital and the cost of capital.

That makes long-term revenue certainty more valuable than exposure to short-term wholesale prices. The bottleneck is now risk allocation: which risks private capital can price efficiently, and which simply return later as higher bids and higher bills when left in the market.

For developers, lenders and utilities, the competitive edge is moving towards regulatory fit and financing structure. For market-makers, important decisions must be made regarding adding further bespoke support regimes, or starting to simplify the toolkit.

In the beginning…

Britain's electricity market was originally built for a system in which fuel-burning plants did most of the work. Wholesale energy prices were strongly shaped by the marginal cost of thermal generation, especially gas. A generator's economics were therefore tied to the same commodity that helped set its revenue. As such, when fuel costs moved, power prices moved with them, and the market could still function.

This works less well with the modern generation mix. Wind, solar and much of nuclear are capital-intensive and operationally light. Most of the cost arrives at the start, through development, construction and financing. Then once built, the cost of producing an additional unit of electricity (short run marginal cost – SRMC) is low. The old model of relying on the power market for long term returns is challenged because short-term prices linked to global commodity prices are a poor way to repay large upfront capital over 15, 20 or 30 years.

This has led to a rise in regulation in the sector. As the asset base has changed, policymakers feel a need to intervene. The fuel-led system could tolerate greater merchant exposure. A capital-led system struggles to do so without raising the cost of capital, and with it the cost to consumers. Politicians also struggle with high short-term prices for consumers when the marginal cost of generation has not changed, for a substantial proportion of the generation fleet.

Why the economics changed

The key shift is from fuel economics to capital economics. Gas-fired generation has historically been naturally hedged as the plant's cost base and the market price were at least partly correlated. Renewable generation does not enjoy that relationship: An offshore wind farm does not become cheaper or more expensive to run because the power price moves. The wind resource and operational performance matter, as do debt costs and equity returns. But the wholesale price fundamentally lacks much connection to the project's underlying cost.

This creates a financing problem. A lender or investor looking at a long-lived renewable asset needs confidence that revenues will be stable enough to recover capital. Corporate power purchase agreements can help in individual cases, but they cannot be the template for an entire national system. At a system level, some form of regulatory wrapper becomes the substitute for the missing link between asset cost and market revenue.

As such, the more generation comes from assets with low SRMC and high upfront costs, the more the state is drawn into underwriting revenue certainty, shaping planning and deciding which risks should remain private.

How the regulatory toolkit actually works

Britain has responded not with one model, but with a layered toolkit. Whilst these models differ in design, they serve a common purpose: they decide which risks remain with the project and which are socialised because private capital would price them too expensively.

The earliest renewable support models were the Renewables Obligation Certificate, or ROC, and the feed-in tariff, or FiT. A ROC gave eligible renewable generators a certificate on top of the power they sold, that could itself be traded to create a premium over wholesale revenues. A FiT worked more directly: smaller eligible generators received a set payment for the electricity they produced, and often a separate payment for power exported to the grid, giving them a clearer and more predictable income stream. Both were, in effect, support mechanisms rather than pure market arrangements. These worked in a younger industry where support was needed mainly to make projects economic in the first place.

As technologies matured, the requirement changed. The issue was less whether a project needed outright subsidy, and more whether it needed predictable income. The Contract for Difference, or CfD, addressed that problem. Under a CfD, a project is effectively given a fixed strike price for its output over a defined term. If the market price falls below that level, the project receives a top-up. If the market price rises above it, the project pays back the difference. This swaps volatile merchant revenues for a bankable cash-flow profile that can support cheaper finance.

The move from ROC to CfD marked a transition from subsidising immature generation to stabilising the revenue stack of capital-heavy generation. Competition inside CfD auctions pushed prices down, especially in offshore wind, by letting developers compete on cost while removing part of the revenue uncertainty.

The same logic appears in other parts of the system. A regulated asset base, or RAB, is a model in which an asset owner is allowed to earn a regulated return on an approved capital base over time. It is common in network sectors such as water and transmission. In energy, it has become especially relevant for assets whose scale, long life and strategic importance make pure merchant exposure too difficult. A full project RAB pushes more risk away from investors and towards consumers, but can reduce financing costs significantly.

Cap and floor is another variant. It is used for interconnectors, and long duration electricity storage (LDES). A floor gives investors downside protection if revenues are too low, whilst the cap limits windfall gains if the revenues are very high. The result is neither pure market exposure nor fixed return, but a bounded range that makes financing possible.

The emergence of technology-by-technology regulation

As risks differ technology-by-technology, the sector has become regulated technology-by-technology.

Offshore wind, negative pricing, and CfDs

For offshore wind, the main risks are construction cost, financing cost, wind resource, operational availability and market design. Exposure to wholesale power prices is not useful, as the physical asset will generate when the wind blows regardless of whether short-term prices happen to be high or low.

This raises a challenge for market-makers – if power-price exposure does not change operational behaviour much, why leave it in the project? This question sits behind growing unease about rule changes inside the CfD regime, including the treatment of prolonged negative pricing.

Over the past few years, periods of negative pricing have increased. Under the CfD regime, difference payments are suspended during sustained periods of negative pricing under specific contract periods. That may help allay criticisms that subsidised generators are still paid through sustained oversupply, but it also creates new complications. If a policy instrument is meant to reduce revenue risk, but then reintroduces chunks of that risk through secondary design choices, bidders will simply price that uncertainty back into the strike price – and the consumer may still pay.

Interconnectors

Interconnectors sit somewhere between network and market assets. Their revenues depend on arbitrage between jurisdictions, which is too uncertain to fund comfortably on a pure merchant basis over several decades. Cap and floor has therefore become a practical compromise.

Long-duration energy storage

LDES is now moving from policy debate into regulated delivery. Shorter-duration batteries can often trade on wholesale volatility, balancing spreads and ancillary-service revenues. Much longer-duration assets increasingly look like strategy system infrastructure that may be critical to system resilience but too exposed to merchant risk to finance cheaply on that basis alone. This is why the UK has now confirmed a cap-and-floor regime, which is now being implemented, giving projects a degree of downside protection whilst limiting windfall upside.

Nuclear

Nuclear goes further still. Its economics are dominated by capital intensity, construction complexity and long horizons, making a standard merchant framework inadequate. Hinkley Point C received a CfD whilst Sizewell has moved towards a RAB model. Challenges here show the limits of market pricing for very large capital projects.

Gas

Historically, gas set prices, ran frequently and covered costs through energy sales. In a renewables-heavy system, this logic weakens. Gas plants may remain essential for security of supply, but if utilisation becomes too low, merchant revenues are unlikely to sustain it. The capacity market was originally designed to pay for plant availability, rather than output, but there is mounting pressure on whether that is sufficient for a fleet that may run rarely yet remain critical. As such, increasingly even gas looks like an asset being paid for its role in the system, rather than for producing routine power.

The live policy argument

Britain now clearly has a crowded menu: merchant exposure, legacy support models, CfDs, cap and floor, competition proxies and full project RAB. Too many models can create confusion about which risk sits where, weaken comparability across technologies, and allow inefficiencies to hide inside bespoke structures.

This is natural in a market that has gone through immense change over the last twenty years. Key next steps for policy makers are to do two things at once: Ensure that the toolkit now in play is used effectively, and address the fundamental risks that the market is unable to price.

The effect of wider market interventions also needs to be considered as, without this, investors have to price in these potential and undefined downside risks, pushing up finance costs.

Next steps

Whilst the direction of travel is more regulation in aggregate, it is not necessarily needed in every corner. Some areas will require new forms of support, especially where technologies are strategic, capital-intensive or underutilised by design. Long-duration storage was an obvious candidate. Security-of-supply arrangements for thermal backup may be another. More centralised planning is also likely as network constraints and locational decisions become harder to leave to decentralised market signals alone.

At the same time, there is a case for reducing regulation where it adds complexity without lowering system cost. That means fewer overlapping mechanisms, clearer risk allocation and less redesign of support schemes after the fact. The test should be straightforward: if a risk cannot be hedged cheaply by the market and will simply reappear in bids, there is little virtue in pretending otherwise.

In a capital-intensive business, a core metric is (by definition) the cost of capital. The spread above the underlying risk-free rate is therefore a core area that needs to be targeted to lower the overall cost of the energy system to consumers.

That, more than any slogan about markets or subsidies, will determine whether the next phase of power-sector regulation makes the transition cheaper or merely more complicated.

Quick-fire industry review

What is the best near-term commercial opportunity in the space?

Long-duration energy storage, particularly if the support design makes duration risk financeable

What is the single biggest barrier?

Politics, because these are long-lived assets and capital needs a stable policy environment

What policy change would help the industry the most?

Cross party support for CfD auctions paired with tighter control of seabed lease costs

What is the most overhyped misconception today?

That renewables are inherently expensive, when a large share of visible cost is shaped by financing and system design

Which one metric would you track monthly for the industry?

Cost of capital, because in a capex heavy system it tells you whether policy is lowering or raising bills

For those interested, where can they learn more?

Ofgem consultations, decisions and regulatory publications

Thomas Fletcher is a Director in EY's Corporate Finance team. He has 20 years of experience focusing on structuring transactions and raising funds in the Power & Utilities space.
