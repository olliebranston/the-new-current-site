Rob Harris-Deans on data centres and the energy transition

How data centres are moving from digital real estate to energy infrastructure

Sep 2026 · Data centres · Digital infrastructure · Grid connections · Energy transition

Data centres are no longer just digital real estate. Growth in enterprise data, cloud and AI is creating huge demand for new capacity, and the biggest question is increasingly whether the power system can meet this need, globally and in Europe.

That is bringing the sector firmly into the energy-transition conversation. Major data centre customers are pursuing carbon-free power and hybrid procurement models, but those ambitions still have to contend with the physical realities of firm electricity supply, competition for renewable generation, water use and local acceptance.

Grid access is therefore becoming one of the industry's defining constraints. Developers are competing for scarce connections while exploring on-site generation, private wires, private grids, SMRs and greater demand flexibility. Solving these challenges is going to need greater collaboration across the data centre and energy value chain. Those who can do this most effectively will have a true competitive advantage.

What is a data centre?

At their simplest, they are buildings designed to house servers and the mechanical and electrical systems needed to keep them running reliably.

Data centres are not actually one asset class but several. A key distinction is the type of compute or workload they support. Some facilities are geared towards storage; others towards cloud applications, business software or AI workloads. In practice, that creates four broad revenue models: enterprise storage, cloud deployment, AI inference and AI training. All are 'data centres', but they differ materially in power intensity, siting needs and contract structure.

What they all have in common is the need for reliable power. Data centres are high-load, always-on assets, so operators need confidence that firm power will be available when required. That requirement sits at the heart of both the engineering and commercial model.

Land and power constraints are not new to the European data centre market. Established hubs such as Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin have faced challenges securing credible power solutions for years.

How AI is changing things – physical, location and contractual differences

Cloud and enterprise data growth were already pushing the data centre market forward, in particular, in metro markets. These drivers still exist, but AI has accelerated demand and changed its shape.

The first change is physical. AI training and inference increasingly rely on high-powered GPUs from NVIDIA and others. It means higher power consumption per server rack, and pushes operators towards liquid cooling mechanisms and the resulting higher build costs.

The second change is location. While certain types of AI inference will continue to be located in core metro markets and existing cloud availability zones, large scale AI training, batch inference and less latency-sensitive inference use cases (e.g. image generation) do not require close proximity to the end-user. They are being served by a new wave of large-scale remote data centre campuses targeting locations with available on-site power generation or grid connectivity drawn to existing or planned power infrastructure.

The third is commercial. Traditional cloud deployments are typically supported by long-term commitments within established availability-zone architectures, with a relatively high expectation of renewal or follow-on demand. Neoclouds are now also buying large-scale AI capacity, but generally bring a less established business model, less balance sheet strength and, in some cases, shorter commitments.

Where this fits in the climate transition

With continued cloud and AI demand growth, the underlying data centre market remains strong. But that growth is creating a significant challenge for the energy sector. Meeting it will require large-scale investment in generation and transmission across a wide range of markets.

The difficulty is that the two industries operate on different investment cycles and carry different risks. Utilities and energy investors need confidence that demand will materialise before committing capital, while data centre developers need confidence that power will arrive before committing to a site. That creates questions around counterparty risk and stranded assets, but also opportunities for new financing and partnership models.

Data centres are therefore becoming an increasingly important part of the energy-transition story, as digital demand starts to shape grid expansion, power procurement and the case for new low-carbon generation. Major tenants are also pursuing net-zero targets through renewable procurement, power purchase agreements and hourly-matching strategies. But financially supporting clean generation is not the same as physically powering a specific site with low-carbon electricity in every hour of the day.

The key levers are therefore grid investment, connection reform, generation build-out and the rules governing private power arrangements. Meeting the scale of demand expected over the next decade will require much greater convergence between tenants, data centre operators, utilities, power generators, investors and regulators.

What the future looks like

The relationship will continue to work both ways, with the procurement behaviour of data centre customers acting as the stimulus to fund low-carbon power generation and transmission infrastructure, while the quality and condition of the power system will determine where data centre capacity can actually be built.

Players across the value chain will pull on a mesh of different power options to deliver the required baseloads, adapt data centre operations to operate within the constraints and use levers to gain acceptance from the community.

Private wires and on-site generation

Private wires can connect a data centre directly to nearby generation or another grid connection point, potentially reducing reliance on constrained public networks. On-site generation and hybrid microgrids can play a similar role where conventional grid upgrades would take too long, potentially acting as power redundancy once grid connection is achieved.

This is also bringing power generators and utilities further into the data centre value chain, through powered-land sales, joint ventures and other development partnerships. Clear rules around regulation, liabilities and cost allocation will be important to promote investment in projects of this nature.

A portfolio approach to power

Most large campuses will need a portfolio rather than a single source of power. That may include grid supply, wind or solar, battery storage, back-up systems and, where available, firm low-carbon power. An increased go-to-market focus of power generation players is to target local data centre developments, alongside greater use of batteries to smooth peaks, support power quality and help manage intermittency. Batteries do not remove the need for dependable supply or grid connection over long periods, but allow optimisation of cost, reliability and carbon footprint together.

Small modular reactors

Small modular reactors are attractive on paper because they promise firm, low-carbon power close to large loads. That is exactly what many data-centre campuses want. But they are not a near-term fix for today's connection queues. Technology maturity, regulation, licensing, supply chains and delivery timelines all still matter. SMRs may become part of the medium- to long-term toolkit, but they should not be treated as the answer to every current bottleneck.

Demand flexibility

There is some room for compute-side flexibility: internal processing scheduled for quieter periods or balancing compute workloads across multiple facilities for tenants with a portfolio infrastructure footprint. AI training and batch processing may offer more opportunity for flexibility than live inference.

Waste heat, water stewardship and local acceptance

As data centres become larger, working with local communities and demonstrating a clear local benefit is increasingly important. Waste heat can support local heat networks, industrial users or greenhouses where there is a practical nearby offtaker. Water can be contentious, with greater use of closed-loop liquid cooling recirculating coolant offering a potential solution.

The wider local bargain may include jobs, grid investment, heat reuse, water commitments and support for local infrastructure. Projects that can show a credible local contribution will have a stronger case with planners and communities.

Next steps

Three questions matter most from here. First, can AI monetisation keep pace with the capital being deployed in its name? Second, can grids, regulation and private infrastructure solutions scale quickly enough to stop power from becoming the permanent bottleneck? Third, which markets create the most credible environment for coordination between digital demand and energy-system delivery?

For utilities, grid operators, policymakers and developers, the decision now is whether to treat data-centre growth as a speculative tech story or as critical infrastructure planning. The markets that answer the question fastest will capture the next wave of investment.

Quick-fire industry review

What is the best near-term commercial opportunity in the space?

Secured powered land in a core European market.

What is the single biggest barrier?

Access to firm, deliverable power.

What policy change would help the industry the most?

A stable framework around grid investment, private wires and longer-term power solutions.

What is the most overhyped misconception today?

That all data centres are basically the same.

Which one metric would you track monthly for the industry?

Deliverable, contracted capacity with credible customers and a realistic route to power.

Rob Harris-Deans is a Director in EY-Parthenon's Telecommunications, Media and Technology practice, with a focus on Digital Infrastructure. Over the past 15 years, he has worked on projects in due diligence, corporate strategy, and investment prioritization with clients across the Telecommunications and Digital Infrastructure space. Prior to EY-Parthenon, he worked in Boston for CMA, conducting in-depth economic and financial analysis on assets.
