Tom Morris on the Behind the Meter opportunity

How distributed energy resources are moving from green add-ons to growth-enabling infrastructure

Sep 2026 · Behind-the-meter energy · Distributed energy · Grid connections · Energy transition

Behind the Meter (BtM) assets are moving from a sustainability add-on to strategic infrastructure. For commercial and industrial users, the business case is driven by three converging pressures: grid connections that arrive too slowly, a higher premium on resilience and power quality, and the growing role of non-energy charges in electricity bills.

The core mechanism is straightforward: If a site can generate, store or shift power at the right time, it can stay within its existing connection, reduce exposure to volatile imports and protect operations from outages.

The catch is that this remains a site-specific market; electrical configuration, available space and load profile still determine what is viable. For retailers, manufacturers and data-centre developers, BtM assets are increasingly less about marginal efficiency gains and more about securing capacity, continuity and cost control in a more constrained power system.

Where Behind the Meter fits in the energy transition

Behind the Meter (BtM) assets sit on the customer side of the connection point, where generation, storage and flexible demand can reshape how a site uses electricity without waiting for the whole system to change. Their role in the transition is not to replace the grid, but to complement it; reducing transported energy at the most stressed times and giving end users more control over cost, continuity and carbon.

That matters in a system under growing strain. Electricity networks are being asked to absorb more demand, more variable renewable generation and more complex operating conditions. BtM assets help by shifting part of the balancing task closer to the point of use. A more decentralised system is not automatically a simpler one, but where on-site assets are well deployed, it can be a more flexible and more manageable one.

Decentralisation vs Capacity

BtM is often framed as a decentralisation play. In practice, for many commercial and industrial users, it is increasingly a capacity solution.

A site's connection agreement sets a maximum import capacity. If a supermarket wants to add rapid electric vehicle charging, or a warehouse wants to electrify part of its operations, increasing that ceiling may trigger local reinforcement works and a queue that runs for years. The UK Government revealed that speculative applications drove the transmission demand queue up by 460 per cent in the six months to June 2025, contributing to waits of up to 15 years for some projects. On-site solar and battery storage can instead shave the demand peak, allowing the site to do more within its existing connection.

This is what makes BtM commercially significant. The value is not just lower energy costs, it is earlier access to growth. A retailer that installs charging ahead of local network upgrades can capture footfall sooner. A data-centre developer that secures 'speed to power' can move ahead of competing sites still waiting on broader grid works. This shifts the question from whether on-site assets are a useful efficiency tool to whether waiting for the grid now carries a material opportunity cost.

Security of supply builds the business case

Physical resilience

The grid is operating in a tougher environment: older assets, more variable generation, more electrified loads and harsher weather. This puts increased pressure on power quality and has increasingly resulted in problems for customers. The cyber backdrop is also an increasing concern, with roughly four nationally significant incidents a week.

The economic damage from an outage can far exceed the electricity bill itself, particularly for critical infrastructure and process-heavy industry. The practical question for a board is not whether a rare event sounds improbable, but what one second, one hour or one day without stable power would actually cost. BtM assets can then be judged against a real continuity requirement, while still lowering costs in normal operation.

That is why resilience increasingly strengthens the business case rather than sitting beside it. A battery or on-site generation asset does not need to justify itself only through avoided imports. If it also reduces the risk of downtime, contractual penalties or operational disruption, the commercial logic becomes materially stronger.

Price security and energy costs

Physical resilience is only one side of security of supply – the other is price security. Two energy crises in four years have made clear that volatility is becoming a structural feature of the market. Geopolitics can still move prices sharply, as can the growing role of intermittent renewables, which change when power is abundant and when it is scarce. Whilst geopolitical events have triggered all-time highs for fossil fuels over the past few years, Great Britain also saw 149 hours of negative pricing in 2024 and 112 of them in Q2 2025 alone.

However, many businesses still procure energy in relatively static terms. BtM assets offer a more active hedge; a site that can generate or discharge during expensive periods reduces its exposure to volatile imports. Similarly, a site with greater control over when it consumes power can respond more intelligently to dynamic price signals. This does not remove volatility from the system but gives the customer more tools to deal with it.

The other shift is in the structure of the bill. Customers do not only pay for electricity itself, but for networks, policy mechanisms and wider system costs. As the grid requires more investment, those non-energy charges become more material. BtM assets' value is not just avoiding a unit of purchased electricity – it is avoiding the system costs embedded in that purchase.

Looking to the future

New value streams

As outlined, the majority of the value from BtM usually comes from avoided cost rather than new revenue. Local flexibility markets, ancillary services and arbitrage can all add value, but they are often secondary. The foundation tends to be lower import costs, reduced peak demand charges, avoided non-energy costs and protection against operational disruption. Projects should not purely be assessed on how much revenue the asset can earn, but how much cost, risk and delay it can remove from a specific site.

This nuance also helps explain why commercial models are evolving: Many businesses do not want to buy hardware and then manage the complexity of power markets themselves. More suppliers, developers and financiers are moving towards energy-as-a-service models, where the upfront capital is carried by a third party and repaid through contracted savings or service fees.

The value chain remains fragmented, spanning original equipment manufacturers, engineering and construction firms, control software providers, optimisers, financiers and operations teams. Over time, the likely winners will be those who can package these layers into a proposition that feels commercially simple, even if the technicals behind it remain intricate.

New technologies

For most users today, the most relevant technologies remain solar panels, battery energy storage systems (BESS) and, in some cases, combined heat and power. Costs are falling (BESS prices dropped around 40% YoY last year), deployment is becoming more common and the use cases are now better understood. Technology also continues to improve – solar's assumed efficiency ceiling shifted from a long-held 29-30 per cent practical limit to 35 per cent class results this year.

Data centres play an important role in this build out. Whilst they currently only account for about 2-3 per cent of demand in Europe, their impact is highly concentrated. Their demand for fast, resilient and relatively clean power is bringing more capital into on-site energy solutions and making a broader set of technologies commercially interesting. That could accelerate longer-duration storage options such as flow batteries or iron-air systems. Fuel cells and other forms of cleaner dispatchable on-site power may also play a role where resilience requirements are more significant.

There is also growing interest in simpler residential formats, including plug-in solar and, eventually, potentially plug-in storage. This is more relevant to the household market than heavy commercial users, but it shows the direction of travel: As costs fall and installation becomes easier, behind-the-meter assets become less exotic and more accessible.

What to watch

The next phase of the market will depend on how quickly BtM assets become easier to deploy.

Connection reform matters because it could formalise the role of on-site assets in easing grid bottlenecks. Tariff reform matters because clearer dynamic price signals would reward flexibility more directly. Commercial standardisation matters because businesses are far more likely to adopt these systems when they can buy them as a service rather than as a bespoke engineering project.

Subject to these reforms, the case could also be weakened. Changes in tariff design could reduce the value of peak avoidance. Facilitated grid buildout would reduce the urgency in some segments. However, at present, it seems likely that BtM power will continue to move from a peripheral efficiency play to core infrastructure strategy, particularly for sites where growth, resilience and electricity costs now intersect.

Quick-fire industry review

What is the best near-term commercial opportunity in the space?

Avoiding rising non-energy costs and peak imports through on-site solar and storage, especially where a site can unlock more load without a larger connection.

What is the single biggest barrier?

Technical feasibility – every site has its own electrical setup, space constraints and load profile, which makes deployment inherently bespoke.

What policy change would help the industry the most?

Make dynamic or time-of-use tariffs the default, with fixed tariffs available by opt-out, to enhance consumer awareness of flexibility benefits.

What is the most overhyped misconception today?

That behind-the-meter assets mainly make money by exporting electricity. In most cases, the core value is avoided cost, resilience and hedging volatility.

Which one metric would you track monthly for the industry?

Peak site import, or sustained reductions in site import against baseline. It is a practical signal that on-site assets are doing useful work.
