ESG regulation and the climate transition

What ESG regulation actually fixes, where it struggles and why consistency matters

Ollie Branston · 23 October 2026

ESG · Regulation · Sustainability reporting · ISSB

ESG regulation is moving from light-touch corporate reporting into market infrastructure. Its job is to make environmental impacts, workforce and supply-chain practices, and governance risks measurable. Once those issues are comparable, investors, customers and boards can act before those risks arrive in operating results. The strongest case for regulation is that markets often under-price long-term externalities and over-reward short-term performance.

Firms push back when transparency becomes prescription. Disclosure can improve capital allocation, but due diligence rules, origin requirements and border measures start to shape business models directly. This can create debates over cost, intrusiveness and competitive advantage.

2026 has shown the bigger risk is inconsistency and instability: The EU cut the scope of its flagship reporting rules about a year after the first reports were published, whilst the UK issued its own version of the global ISSB (International Sustainability Standards Board) standards with local amendments. For boards and investors, the open question is whether ISSB-based standards become a common baseline, or whether national tailoring and repeated redesign turn ESG into another fragmented compliance stack.

What ESG regulation actually is

Environmental, social and governance (ESG) regulation is an umbrella term for rules that require companies to report, evidence or change how they manage environmental, social and governance issues. This article focuses mainly on environmental regulation, but the wider category also includes supply-chain labour standards, human-rights due diligence, governance controls and broader non-financial reporting. Regulation can take the form of light touch reporting, by just asking firms to disclose information, or more interventionist requirements like proof of product origin, supplier examination, and commercial behaviour adjustment.

Where this fits in the climate transition

ESG regulation is a governance layer that forces companies to measure climate, nature and supply-chain effects that standard financial accounting may miss. By setting standards around reporting, and performance within said reporting, this regulation can drive change. Its effectiveness depends on usable data, comparable standards and enough policy stability for markets to act on the signal rather than wait out the next overhaul.

Correcting a market blind spot

In practice, ESG regulation means listed companies and larger private groups must disclose more about their non-financial performance: Environmental impacts, social effects and how they govern themselves. This aims to increase corporate accountability – companies are still judged on earnings, return on capital or share price, but are increasingly also asked what wider impacts their operations and products create, and what those impacts may do to the business in return.

Governments move from voluntary frameworks to mandatory rules for two related reasons. The first is short-termism: Markets often reward near-term performance, yet transition investments and resilience plans pay back over much longer periods. The second is comparability: In a global market, different norms on pollution, labour or sourcing can produce large cost gaps. Mandatory disclosure is an attempt to stop these externalities sitting permanently off-balance-sheet.

Political pressure comes from both voters and capital. Public concern about pollution, climate and nature has made it harder for governments to rely on voluntary action alone. At the same time, long-term asset owners such as pension funds and insurers increasingly treat climate risk as portfolio risk rather than a moral overlay. This combination explains why ESG regulation has moved out of niche compliance territory and into mainstream corporate reporting.

From disclosure baseline to due diligence

On paper, disclosure looks administrative. In practice, it is a baseline measurement system. The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) offered a useful example: By forcing companies to report climate risks and likely financial impacts through a common lens, it let investors compare peers on something more substantive than green claims: how firms identify physical and transition risk, how exposed their products and suppliers are and what opportunities they are pursuing. The TCFD has since been wound up and its framework carried into the ISSB’s climate standard.

ESG regulation has a broad remit which falls into two buckets. The first is softer, light touch reporting disclosures: climate risk reporting, broader sustainability disclosures, supply-chain obligations, human-rights checks, country-of-origin expectations and green taxes or border measures. More interventionist, steering-oriented rules sit in a second bucket, where regulators try to shape behaviour more directly.

The expected corporate pushback on intrusiveness

Disclosure rules create work but still leave management teams with discretion. Harder pushback appears when regulation dictates how firms run parts of the business: How deeply to examine suppliers, how often to carry out diligence, or how much origin detail to gather across a chain. For companies, this can feel less like transparency and more like operating instruction.

This was to be expected. Financial reporting has had decades to build processes, definitions and audit disciplines, and still produces restatements. By comparison, non-financial reporting is trying to build equivalent infrastructure at speed, in the context of the urgency of the environmental, social and governance issues faced. As such, first-wave reporters under the EU’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) were never going to find a settled system waiting for them. Interpretation varies across sectors, firms and jurisdictions, data is patchy, controls are immature and assurance is still developing. However, difficulties should not lead to too much pessimism – even financial reporting took roughly 40 years to spread IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) widely, and the US still retained its own GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) framework.

As such, regulators should not be too disheartened. Relative to the machinery already devoted to financial reporting, ESG disclosure is still modest. The more important test is whether the information changes behaviour. On climate risk, supply-chain resilience and long-term capital planning, it increasingly does. The burden debate matters, but companies have long disclosed exhaustive detail on returns while saying much less about the externalities that shape those returns over time.

Global consistency is economically desirable and politically difficult

Over the past 10 to 15 years, the EU has been the main rule-maker in this space, driven by electorates that treat pollution, nature and carbon as active political issues. It is now also a clear example of the instability problem - the Omnibus I package, adopted in February, let member states exempt first-wave companies that fall outside the scope from reporting and dropped the EU-wide civil liability scheme, about a year after first reports were published.

Elsewhere, the picture is looser: US political change has pulled back parts of the previous federal sustainability agenda, while other jurisdictions remain a patchwork of faster and slower approaches. In the UK, the FCA consulted on making climate disclosures mandatory for listed companies, then settled on comply-or-explain. Broadly, the direction of travel still favours more disclosure and more scrutiny, but the pace is uneven and the rules keep moving.

Most companies would benefit from lower compliance costs and more comparability, but international negotiations are difficult. Technical differences on materiality, assurance or reporting boundaries matter, but the deeper dispute is over timing and fairness. Countries enter negotiations from very different income levels, industrial structures and domestic pressures. What looks like sensible climate discipline in one market can look like a threat to affordability, jobs or development in another.

This tension is clearest in trade-exposed sectors. A region trying to accelerate lower-carbon steel may see border measures as a way to stop cleaner domestic producers being undercut. An emerging exporter may see the same tool as a rich-market device designed to neutralise its existing cost advantage. North-south politics sharpens the point. Wealthier economies industrialised under looser environmental constraints and are now asking poorer ones to move faster. So the global challenge is not only technical harmonisation. It is whether the economics of transition can be distributed in a way that other countries accept as fair.

What better looks like

The most plausible route out of this fragmentation is convergence around the ISSB’s IFRS S1 and S2 standards. These can create a common reporting architecture that global businesses can build once, govern properly and use across markets. For boards, finance teams and investors, consistency is usually more valuable than another round of bespoke national refinements. There is momentum on this – by July 2026 35 jurisdictions had adopted the standards on a voluntary or mandatory basis. The UK also published its own versions, UK SRS S1 and S2, in February 2026.

The balance must be found between tailoring standards to local market preferences without breaking comparability. The UK standards already depart from the global baseline in places, removing first-year transitional relief and making industry-based guidance optional. Each change may be sensible on its own, but local amendments are how comparability erodes.

As such, regulators should err on the side of less amendment. Companies can adapt to strict rules, but struggle to plan around continual change of the kind Europe has recently demonstrated. That kind of fragmentation increases cost, slows investment and weakens the signal the rules were meant to create.

A likely next phase is calibration. Timelines may move, and some requirements may be simplified or made more intuitive. However, the underlying logic should survive to ensure businesses understand resource constraints, physical climate risk and supply-chain fragility before those issues arrive in operating results.
